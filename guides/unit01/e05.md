Whenever you turn a volume knob, you are operating an electronic device called a *rheostat*. We attach knobs to rheostats to create volume controls. We usually number these controls from one to ten, though other approaches exist.

![[e05_01.png]]

In this lab, we’ll attach a rheostat to an Arduino and use it to control a speaker's pitch.

# How does a rheostat work?

The circuit below demonstrates that the voltage across a single resistor goes from the maximum voltage (5V) to the minimum (GND or 0V).

![[e05_02.png]]

The circuit to the left has one resistor, and the 5V from the power supply drops to zero volts as the current moves through the resistor.

However, the 5V does not become 0V all at once. The voltage drops as current moves through the resistor. If you could look at the voltage halfway through the resistor, you would see 2.5V. If you looked at the voltage ⅘ of the way through the resistor, you would see 1 V.

A rheostat allows you to look at the voltage partway across a resistor and use the voltage to do something, such as control the volume on your amplifier or the pitch on your speaker.

## A rheostat in a circuit diagram

A rheostat (also called a *potentiometer*, but that is too long) in a circuit diagram looks like a resistor with an arrow showing the point where we are pulling out the voltage:

In our lab, the voltage tap will go into an analog pin so we can read its value and use it. Other circuits may attach the output voltage to other components.

## The rheostat in our electronic kits

The rheostat in our electronic kits looks like this:

5 VoltsGNDVariable voltage

![[e05_03.png]]

There are three pins. Two of the pins are next to each other. You connect these to 5V and GND. It doesn’t matter which. One pin is offset from the other two. This is the output pin. You read this pin to see the value from the rheostat.
